> Consider this summation $\sum_{i=x}^{y} i^2$.


#### Part A

> Determine the equivalent closed–form expression of this summation. (In this context, ‘closed–form’ means ‘not a summation of $y − x + 1$ terms.’) Include a short explanation of how you created the expression.

-------


The closed-form of the summation $\sum_{i=x}^{y} i^2$ is:

$$
\frac{1}{3}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{6}y - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$


To derive this expression, we start by noting that a sum of powers such as $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2$ is typically a polynomial of degree higher than the power of the summand (in this case, $i^2$ implies a polynomial of degree 3). Assume the form:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{y} i^2 = a_3y^3 + a_2y^2 + a_1y + a_0
$$


For now assume $x=1$ and plug in small values of $y$.

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{1} i^2 = a_3(1)^3 + a_2(1)^2 + a_1(1) + a_0 = a_3 + a_2 + a_1 + a_0 = 1
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{2} i^2 = a_3(2)^3 + a_2(2)^2 + a_1(2) + a_0 = 5a_3 + 4a_2 + 2a_1 + a_0 = 5
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{3} i^2 = a_3(3)^3 + a_2(3)^2 + a_1(3) + a_0 = 14a_3 + 9a_2 + 3a_1 + a_0 = 14
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2 = a_3(4)^3 + a_2(4)^2 + a_1(4) + a_0 = 30a_3 + 16a_2 + 4a_1 + a_0 = 30
$$

We can find the values of $a_3$, $a_2$, $a_1$, and $a_0$ by solving the system of equations using matrices. Form the coefficient matrix $A$ and the constant matrix $B$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
8 & 4 & 2 & 1 \\
27 & 9 & 3 & 1 \\
64 & 16 & 4 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
5 \\
14 \\
30
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$AX = B \Rightarrow X = A^{-1}B$

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix}
a_3 \\
a_2 \\
a_1 \\
a_0
\end{bmatrix} = A^{-1}B = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{1}{3} \\
\frac{1}{2} \\
\frac{1}{6} \\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the closed-form expression of the summation $\sum_{i=1}^{y} i^2$ is:

$$
\frac{1}{3}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{6}y
$$

When the lower bound of the summation is $x$ instead of $1$, we are essentially subtracting the sum of the first $x-1$ terms from the sum of the first $y$ terms.

The sum of the first $y$ terms was found previously:

$$
S_y = \frac{1}{3}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{6}y
$$

The sum of the first $x-1$ terms is:

$$
S_{x-1} = \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1)
$$

Therefore, the sum of the terms from $x$ to $y$ is:

$$
S_y - S_{x-1} = \frac{1}{3}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{6}y - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$

#### Part B

> Prove that your answer to (b) is correct

----


**Proof by Induction on $y \geq x$**

***Base Case***: $y = x$

$$
\sum_{i=x}^{x} i^2 = x^2 = \frac{1}{3}x^3 + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{6}x - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$

$$
x^2 = \frac{1}{3}x^3 + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{6}x - \left( \frac{1}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{3}x^2 + \frac{1}{2}x^2 - \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{6}x - \frac{1}{6} \right)
$$

$$
x^2 = x^2
$$

***Inductive Hypothesis***: Assume the statement is true for $y = k$

$$
\sum_{i=x}^{k} i^2 = \frac{1}{3}k^3 + \frac{1}{2}k^2 + \frac{1}{6}k - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$

***Inductive Step***: Prove the statement is true for $y = k+1$ given the statement is true for $y = k$


WTSP $\sum_{i=x}^{k+1} i^2 = \frac{1}{3}(k+1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(k+1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(k+1) - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)$

$$
\sum_{i=x}^{k+1} i^2 = \sum_{i=x}^{k} i^2 + (k+1)^2
$$

Plug in the inductive hypothesis:

$$
\frac{1}{3}k^3 + \frac{1}{2}k^2 + \frac{1}{6}k - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right) + (k+1)^2
$$

Expand $(k+1)^2$:

$$
\frac{1}{3}k^3 + \frac{1}{2}k^2 + \frac{1}{6}k - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right) + k^2 + 2k + 1
$$

Combine the $k^2$, $2k$, and constant terms:

$$
\frac{1}{3}k^3 + (1 + \frac{1}{2})k^2 + (\frac{1}{6} + 2)k + 1 - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$

This simplifies to:

$$
\frac{1}{3}(k+1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(k+1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(k+1) - \left( \frac{1}{3}(x-1)^3 + \frac{1}{2}(x-1)^2 + \frac{1}{6}(x-1) \right)
$$

This is exactly the closed-form expression we derived in Part A. Therefore, if the statement is true for $y = k$, then it is true for $y = k+1$. By induction, the statement is true for all $y \geq x$. $\blacksquare$

#### Part A

> $E(n) = 4n^2$


----

The execution time on Computer B for the same problem size $n$ would be $\frac{E(n)}{32}$. We want to find the largest problem size $n$ such that the execution time on Computer B is equal to the execution time on Computer A such that:

For computer A, the time to solve the problem of size $n$ is: $E(n) = cn^2 = 4n^2$.

For Computer B, which is 32 times faster, it can solve a problem of size $n_b$ in the same time, so the relationship is:

$$
E(n_b) = 32E(n) \Rightarrow 4n_b^2 = 32(4n^2) 
$$

Cancel out the 4s:

$$
n_b^2 = 32n^2
$$

Square root both sides:

$$
n_b = \sqrt{32}n = 4\sqrt{2}n
$$

Therefore, Computer B can solve a problem of size $4\sqrt{2}n$ in the same time as Computer A can solve a problem of size $n$.

#### Part B

> $E(n) = 3^n$


----

For Computer A, the time to solve the problem of size $n$ is: $E(n) = 3^n$.

For Computer B, which is 32 times faster, it can solve a problem of size $n_b$ in the same time, so the relationship is:

$$
E(n_b) = 32E(n) \Rightarrow 3^{n_b} = 32(3^n)

$$
3^{n_b} = 3^{5}3^n = 3^{n+5}

\log_3 3^{n_b} = \log_3 3^{n+5}

> Using the definition of Big–O presented in class, show that f (n) = 100 is O(1) and also O(n2). (Need the definition? See the slides of Sept. 12th.)


*Definition of Big-O*: 

> Let $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ be functions s.t.($f,g : \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$).
> 
> We say that $f(n)$ is $O(g(n))$ (or $f(n) \in O(g(n))$) if **there exists** a real constant $c > 0$ and and **there exists** an integer constant $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $0 \leq f(n) \leq cg(n)$ for all $n \geq n_0$.


----

**Proof that $f(n) = 100$ is $O(1)$**

Let $g(n) = 1$. We need to find a constant $c > 0$ and an integer constant $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $0 \leq 100 \leq c$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Let $c = 100$ and $n_0 = 1$. Then $0 \leq 100 \leq 100$ for all $n \geq 1$. Therefore, $f(n) = 100$ is $O(1)$.

**Proof that $f(n) = 100$ is $O(n^2)$**

Let $g(n) = n^2$. We need to find a constant $c > 0$ and an integer constant $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $0 \leq 100 \leq cn^2$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Let $c = 1$. Then, we need $100 \leq n^2$ for all $n \geq n_0$. This is true for all $n \geq 10$. Therefore, $f(n) = 100$ is $O(n^2)$.

## Question 7

> Write an $O(n)$ method (in your choice of Java or pseudocode) that accepts references to two doubly–linked lists of integers (both in ascending order) and returns a reference to a new doubly–linked list, also in ascending order, that contains all of the values of the two input lists. Include an explanation of why it is O(n).

```java
public static DoublyLinkedList merge(DoublyLinkedList list1, DoublyLinkedList list2) {
    DoublyLinkedList merged = new DoublyLinkedList();
    Node current1 = list1.head;
    Node current2 = list2.head;

    while (current1 != null && current2 != null) {
        if (current1.data <= current2.data) {
            merged.add(current1.data);
            current1 = current1.next;
        } else {
            merged.add(current2.data);
            current2 = current2.next;
        }
    }

    // Add remaining nodes from list1 (if any)
    while (current1 != null) {
        merged.add(current1.data);
        current1 = current1.next;
    }

    // Add remaining nodes from list2 (if any)
    while (current2 != null) {
        merged.add(current2.data);
        current2 = current2.next;
    }

    return merged;
}
```

The time complexity of this method is $O(n)$ where $n$ is the total number of nodes in the two input lists. The method iterates through the two input lists exactly once, adding each node to the merged list. The time complexity is linear with respect to the total number of nodes in the input lists, making it $O(n)$.

## Question 8

> For each of the pairs of functions given below, is $f(n) \in O(g(n))$, $f(n) \in \Omega(g(n))$, or $f(n) \in \Theta(g(n))$? Use limits to determine your answers (as presented in class, and also in Shaffer Section 3.4.5)

To solve this problem, we first find the limit of the ratio of $f(n)$ to $g(n)$ as $n$ approaches infinity.

- If $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = 0$
  - $f(n) \in O(g(n))$. $f(n)$ grows slower than $g(n)$ as $n$ approaches infinity.
  - $g(n)$ grows asymptotically faster than $f(n)$, so $f(n)$ is upper-bounded by $g(n)$.
- If $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \infty$
  - $f(n) \in \Omega(g(n))$. $f(n)$ grows faster than $g(n)$ as $n$ approaches infinity.
  - $f(n)$ eventually overtakes $g(n)$, so $f(n)$ is lower-bounded by $g(n)$.
- If $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = c$ where $0 < c < \infty$
  - $f(n) \in \Theta(g(n))$. $f(n)$ grows at the same rate as $g(n)$ as $n$ approaches infinity.
  - $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ grow at the same rate, so $f(n)$ is both upper and lower-bounded by $g(n)$.

Find the limit of the ratio of $f(n)$ to $g(n)$ as $n$ approaches infinity:

\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{5n}{\frac{n}{100}} = \lim_{n \to \infty} 500 = 500
$$

Since the limit is a constant $c > 0$, $f(n) \in \Theta(g(n))$.

Since the limit is $\infty$, $f(n) \in \Omega(g(n))$.


## Question 9


> Consider a $\Theta(\log_2 n)$ version of the binary search algorithm that accepts a 1D array of integers (stored in ascending order) plus a search key value, and returns the index of the last occurrence of that key within the array (‘last’ meaning the occurrence with the largest array index). Create this algorithm, in your choice of Java or pseudocode, and include an explanation of why it is $\Theta(\log_2 n)$. 
> 
> Be aware: To achieve the required level of performance, linear search cannot be used.



----



```java
public static int binarySearchLastOccurrence(int[] arr, int key) {
    int low = 0;
    int high = arr.length - 1;
    int result = -1;

    while (low <= high) {
        int mid = low + (high - low) / 2;

        if (arr[mid] == key) {
            result = mid;
            low = mid + 1;
        } else if (arr[mid] < key) {
            low = mid + 1;
        } else {
            high = mid - 1;
        }
    }

    return result;
}
```

The time complexity of this algorithm is $\Theta(\log_2 n)$ where $n$ is the number of elements in the input array.

The algorithm uses a binary search approach to find the last occurrence of the search key within the array.

At each step, the algorithm divides the search space in half, reducing the number of elements to search by half. This logarithmic reduction in the search space results in a time complexity of $\Theta(\log_2 n)$.

$$
T(n) = aT\left(\frac{n}{b}\right) + c \cdot n^d
$$

where:

- $T(n)$ is an increasing function
- $n = b^k$, where $k$ is a non-negative integer
- $a$ is a real and $a \geq 1$
- $b$ is an integer and $b > 1$
- $c$ is a real and $c > 0$
- $d$ is a real and $d \geq 0$

Then:

$$
T(n) = \begin{cases}
\Theta(n^d) & \text{if } d > \log_b a \\
\Theta(n^d \log n) & \text{if } d = \log_b a \\
\Theta(n^{\log_b a}) & \text{if } d < \log_b a
\end{cases}
$$

> $G(i) = 3G(i-3) + k$, assuming that $G(0) = 0$ and $i$ is a multiple of $3$.


----

In the standard form, the problem size decreases by a constant fraction in each step, like $n/b$ where $b > 1$. In this case, the problem size decreases by a constant amount in each step ($i-3$). Therefore, the Master Theorem cannot be applied.
 
Instead, we can solve the recurrence relation by expanding it.

Start with the original recurrence relation:

`
$$
G(i) = 3G(i-3) + k
$$

Find the $G(i-3)$ term:

$$
G(i-3) = 3G(i-6) + k
$$

Substitute this back into the original equation:

$$
G(i) = 3(3G(i-6) + k) + k = 9G(i-6) + 3k + k
$$

Find the $G(i-6)$ term:

$$
G(i-6) = 3G(i-9) + k
$$

Substitute this back into the equation:

$$
G(i) = 9(3G(i-9) + k) + 3k + k = 27G(i-9) + 9k + 3k + k
$$

Find the $G(i-9)$ term:

$$
G(i-9) = 3G(i-12) + k
$$

Substitute this back into the equation:

$$
G(i) = 27(3G(i-12) + k) + 9k + 3k + k = 81G(i-12) + 27k + 9k + 3k + k
$$

This pattern continues, and we can see that the general form of the solution is:

$$
G(i) = 3^kG(i-3k) + k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} 3^j
$$

$$
G(i) = 3^kG(i-3k) + k\left(\frac{3^k - 1}{2}\right)

$$
G(3k) = 3^kG(3k-3k) + k\left(\frac{3^k - 1}{2}\right)

$$
G(3k) = 3^kG(0) + k\left(\frac{3^k - 1}{2}\right)
$$

Since $G(0) = 0$:

$$
G(3k) = k\left(\frac{3^k - 1}{2}\right)
$$

Therefore, the Big-Theta asymptotic solution is $\Theta(3^{i/3})$.
